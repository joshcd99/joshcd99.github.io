Punch Through · LightBlue

Mobile Software Engineer Intern · Minneapolis, MN · Feb 2025 – Aug 2025 · CS / technical

Punch Through is a Minneapolis-based Bluetooth Low Energy consultancy. I worked on LightBlue, their flagship cross-platform BLE companion app used by hardware developers to scan, connect to, and debug BLE peripherals, with 120,000+ monthly active users. My work was a mix of new feature development in SwiftUI/CoreBluetooth and stability work on the parts of CoreBluetooth that hardware engineers depend on every day.

Keywords: iOS, Swift, SwiftUI, CoreBluetooth, BLE, Bluetooth Low Energy, OTA firmware update, embedded systems integration, mobile, consumer-grade app, agile, Xcode.

What I did

  • OTA firmware update workflow. Designed and implemented an over-the-air firmware update flow for client BLE devices in Swift. Reliable distribution with robust error handling and recovery for the edge cases BLE inevitably throws (mid-transfer disconnects, version mismatches, mid-update battery drops).
  • User-facing LightBlue features in SwiftUI / CoreBluetooth. Shipped a favorites system for peripherals (so the same dev kit isn't 30 entries deep on every scan), in-context tooltips, and log filtering. Each one targeted at a concrete pain that hardware engineers had reported.
  • CoreBluetooth stability work. Resolved critical issues across CoreBluetooth scanning, notifications, and connection handling that improved reliability for the 120,000+ MAUs. The kind of fixes that don't ship a feature but stop a thousand support tickets.

Why it matters here

Embedded / BLE work sits at a different layer than my full-stack web work at Great Lakes or the database / RLS work on the capstone. It's the place where the software actually has to negotiate with a physical radio that doesn't care about your error model. That experience (treating the hardware as the ground truth, designing for partial failure, and reading specs more carefully than docs) is one of the most transferable engineering habits I've built. It also pairs unusually well with the industrial-distribution domain at Great Lakes, where the “hardware” on the receiving end is a Zebra scanner or a label printer.
